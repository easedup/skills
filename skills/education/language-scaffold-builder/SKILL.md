# Language Scaffold Builder

You are building scaffolds for a student learning the curriculum and
English at the same time. The goal isn't to translate the lesson into
another language and hand it over — it's to put the student's whole
linguistic repertoire to work alongside English, so the language they
already have becomes a resource for the content, not a barrier this skill
routes around.

---

## Gotchas

- **The EAL/D (or EAL, or ELL) label is not a measure of English
  proficiency.** A student flagged this way might be newly arrived or
  five years into schooling in English — don't default to beginner-level
  scaffolds without asking. Time to full academic proficiency commonly runs
  well past the funding and support windows schools plan around (the Bell
  Foundation's own research on English learners puts it past six years for
  many pupils, against funding models built around three) — a student who
  sounds conversationally fluent may still need heavy academic-language
  scaffolding.
- **Translation and translanguaging are not the same move.** Handing a
  student a translated worksheet replaces English with another language;
  translanguaging uses both together — planning or drafting in the home
  language, then producing English output, or annotating an English text
  with home-language notes. Default to the second unless the teacher
  specifically wants a straight translation.
- **A student who can't yet express something in English may still know
  it.** Where possible, separate checking content knowledge from checking
  English proficiency — a diagram, a home-language explanation, or a
  gesture can be as valid an evidence of understanding as an English
  sentence, and treating it that way is what stops a language gap reading
  as a content gap.

---

## What this skill needs

- Curriculum, year level, teaching context, and the specific task or unit
  this scaffold pack is for. If already known from earlier in this
  conversation, don't ask again.
- Working mode — ask if not already established:
  > "Would you prefer we build this together step by step, or would you
  > like me to ask a few questions and produce a full pack?"

Note the terminology conventionally used for the stated curriculum and
apply it consistently throughout this session:

| Curriculum | Term |
|---|---|
| Australian Curriculum | EAL/D |
| Common Core / US curricula | ELL |
| UK National Curriculum | EAL |

If the curriculum isn't in this table, use "English language learners" as a
neutral term, or mirror whatever term the teacher uses.

---

## Establish curriculum content

Establish the content descriptions for the subject and year level, using
whatever curriculum lookup capability is available in this session. Never
use training data for curriculum standards — if no lookup capability is
available, ask the teacher. This identifies the key vocabulary and
concepts the scaffolds need to carry.

---

## Step 1: Understand the students and the task

Ask together:
> "What's the task or unit this is for? And roughly what phase are these
> students at — still building everyday conversational English, or
> conversational but still developing academic/subject-specific language?
> If you know it and it would help, what home language(s) are represented?
> (Not required — the pack still works without it, just with fewer
> cognate and translation-ready options.)"

Weight scaffolds to the phase named: early-phase students need heavier
visual and non-verbal options and more modelled language; later-phase
students conversational in English typically need academic vocabulary and
text-structure support rather than everyday language support.

**Home language(s):** [If known — otherwise omit this line]

---

**Vocabulary glossary**

| Term | Plain-English definition | Cognate / translation note | Visual cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Key term from curriculum content] | [Simple definition] | [If home language known and a cognate exists, name it — e.g. "Spanish 'fotosíntesis' is a close cognate"; otherwise "Translation slot — fill in with student/family/interpreter if useful"] | [Suggested image, diagram, or gesture to pair with the term — dual-coding, not decoration] |

*(Limit to the terms genuinely needed for this task — a glossary of 30
words is not more useful than one of 8 well-chosen ones.)*

---

**Sentence frames** *(matched to this task's genre — narrative, explanation, argument, procedure, etc.)*

- "[Frame 1 — e.g. 'First, ___ happens. This causes ___, which means ___.']"
- "[Frame 2 — a different structural pattern for the same genre]"
- "[Frame 3 — for a more advanced/later-phase version, less filled-in]"

---

**Dual-coding suggestions**

Pair each key idea with a non-text representation students can use
alongside — not instead of — the written content:
- [e.g. "A labelled diagram of the process, with the glossary terms as
  labels students place themselves"]
- [e.g. "A simple flowchart of the sequence, for students to annotate in
  either language"]

---

**Translanguaging strategies**

- **Planning in home language:** Let students draft notes, a plan, or a
  concept map in their home language before producing English output —
  the thinking happens once, not twice.
- **Annotation:** Accept home-language annotations or margin notes on an
  English text as evidence the student is engaging with it.
- **Paired talk:** If another student shares the home language, allow
  brief home-language discussion of a tricky concept before both return to
  English for the task.
- **Multilingual word walls:** Where a term has a genuine cognate or a
  known translation, display both languages side by side rather than
  English alone.

---

**Checking understanding without relying only on English output**

[1–2 specific suggestions for this task — e.g. "Ask the student to
sequence picture cards showing the process, then describe just one step
in English" rather than requiring a full written explanation from a
student still building academic English]

## Step 3: Family connection (optional)

If this task or unit has a family-facing component:
> "If it would help, I can also put together a short translated summary or
> name an interpreter contact point for families — that's something a
> parent-facing communication skill can build directly from this pack."

---

## Output format

Once finalised, deliver the complete pack in a dedicated standalone
container (e.g., an Artifact, Canvas, or single Markdown code block) rather
than inline chat text. This is a document the teacher will save, adapt, or
use across multiple lessons.

- Only answer inline without a container if the teacher is asking a quick
  conversational question or adjusting a small part of the pack.
- Keep chat text outside the container brief (1–2 sentences confirming
  completion).

## Evidence base
- García, O., Johnson, S.I. & Seltzer, K. (2017), *The Translanguaging
  Classroom* — backs treating a student's full linguistic repertoire as a
  learning resource, the basis for the translanguaging strategies above
  rather than translation alone.
  <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15348458.2021.1930804>
- The Bell Foundation / University of Oxford, *English as an Additional
  Language: Proficiency in English and rate of progression* — backs the
  proficiency-phase caution: many pupils need well beyond the commonly
  funded window to reach full academic English proficiency, so the EAL/D
  label alone doesn't tell you the phase.
  <https://www.bell-foundation.org.uk/news/new-report-proficiency-in-english-is-central-to-understanding-the-educational-attainment-of-learners-using-eal-but-how-long-does-it-take-to-achieve-and-what-support-do-these-learners-need/>
- ACARA, *EAL/D overview and advice* — backs assessing content knowledge
  through means that rely less on English proficiency, and treating
  below-benchmark performance as commensurate with English-acquisition
  phase rather than as underperformance.
  <https://docs.acara.edu.au/resources/EALD_Overview_and_Advice_revised_February_2014.pdf>
- CAST (2024), UDL Guidelines v3.0 — backs the dual-coding suggestions:
  multiple means of representation (visual alongside text) is one of UDL's
  three core principles.
  <https://udlguidelines.cast.org/>
